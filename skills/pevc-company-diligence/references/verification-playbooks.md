# Claim-verification playbooks

Use only the playbooks relevant to the claims that could change the decision. These are tests, not substitute report sections.

## 1. Fact-card handoff and intake reconciliation

1. Import the project fact card and source register when available; confirm versions, cutoff, pages reviewed, and unreadable portions.
2. Reconcile the one-sentence mechanism: the company provides **what**, to **whom**, solves **which problem**, and is paid **how**. Mark missing components as undisclosed.
3. Normalize every material metric into `value / period / unit / definition / actual or forecast / source` and preserve earlier definitions.
4. Carry forward internal conflicts, missing data, and claims requiring external verification rather than silently resolving them.
5. Select the decisive claims to test and record what outcome would change the screening or IC view.

## 2. Customer and order verification

Build a sample that is decision-relevant rather than convenient: top accounts by revenue/economic dependence, recent wins, repeat or expansion accounts, churned/lost accounts, and unusually high- or low-margin accounts.

For each sampled account, trace:

`target → evaluation → pilot/design win → signed order → delivery → acceptance → revenue → collection → repeat/expansion → durable production use`

Cross-check management's customer list against contracts/orders, invoices, acceptance or usage records, receivables and cash receipts, support/warranty logs, and customer interviews as available. Record the highest verified status; do not average multiple weak items into a stronger status.

Interview the right vantage points:

- User: workflow fit, reliability, usage, and switching friction.
- Technical evaluator: benchmark, integration, failure modes, and alternatives.
- Economic buyer: budget, ROI, renewal, and willingness to pay.
- Procurement/payer: contract, tender, discounts, payment, and supplier dependence.

Ask for ranges before point estimates, then request a concrete recent example. Probe what would cause non-renewal, replacement, or volume reduction. Record selection bias and do not rely on a single reference for a material conclusion.

## 3. Public tender and order checks for ToB/ToG

1. Expand entity coverage: target company, former names, subsidiaries, brands, disclosed agents, and major customers.
2. Choose sources by buyer type and geography; record platform and date coverage.
3. Classify each item as procurement intent, tender, candidate, award, contract, amendment, delivery/acceptance, or payment evidence.
4. Map buyer, supplier entity, product, amount, date, contract period, and competing bidders.
5. Reconcile disclosed customers/orders/revenue with public records. Label the result `consistent`, `partly consistent`, `material mismatch`, or `not observable`.
6. Analyze competition using award concentration, price changes, entrant frequency, and regional/customer clustering.

Public sources are incomplete. A search miss is `not observable` unless coverage is sufficiently complete and an affirmative contradiction exists.

## 4. Recurring revenue and SaaS quality

- Rebuild opening ARR + new + expansion − contraction − churn ± FX/definition changes = closing ARR.
- Use customer-level or cohort-level data; reconcile ARR to recognized subscription revenue and billings.
- Separate subscriptions from implementation, services, hardware, pilots, usage overages, and cancellable commitments.
- Calculate gross and net retention with disclosed denominators and period; inspect logo, revenue, and gross-profit retention separately when economics vary.
- Reconcile CAC to fully loaded sales and marketing cost, sales capacity, ramp, payback, and gross margin; do not accept LTV from an assumed perpetual retention rate.
- Compare product usage, support burden, renewal, and expansion to test whether retention is product-led or contract-driven.

## 5. Financial-quality bridges

### Revenue to cash

Reconcile revenue with invoices/billings, deferred revenue or contract liabilities, receivables, write-offs, taxes, and collections. Inspect month-end or quarter-end concentration, aging, subsequent receipts, side letters, rebates, returns, acceptance clauses, and related parties.

### EBITDA to free cash flow

Start with reported EBITDA, show every adjustment, reverse recurring “one-offs,” and subtract cash taxes, interest, working-capital investment, maintenance capex, and recurring capitalized costs. Separate maintenance and growth capex using operational evidence rather than management labels alone.

### Forecast credibility

Compare prior budgets or board plans with actual outcomes by revenue, gross margin, EBITDA, cash, headcount, and key operating drivers. Bridge current forecast growth to installed capacity, sales capacity, signed backlog, historical conversion, delivery schedule, collections, and financing availability.

## 6. Interview calibration and triangulation

Before a full interview slate, use a short calibration conversation or opening block to test whether the respondent has direct, recent, decision-relevant exposure. Record role, employer/context, dates observed, conflicts, and compensation/source.

Move from open description to bounded range, then quantity, then a concrete example and counterexample. Seek disconfirming views and cross-check material claims across independent roles or sources when practical. Never request confidential customer information, trade secrets, or material non-public information.

## 7. Red-flag escalation

For a potential red flag, document:

1. Observation and source.
2. Alternative benign explanations.
3. Mechanism of loss or deal impairment.
4. Evidence that would confirm or clear it.
5. Severity: deal-breaker, significant, or manageable.
6. Consequence for valuation, structure, condition precedent, covenant, representation, holdback, or recommendation.
7. Owner, due date, and current status.

Do not upgrade a suspicion to a fact because management responds slowly. Escalate when the missing or contradictory evidence is itself material to the decision.
