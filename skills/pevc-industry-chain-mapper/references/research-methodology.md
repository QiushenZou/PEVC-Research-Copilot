# Research methodology

## Benchmark-derived design choices

This skill's research sequence was benchmarked against three N.E.I. PEVC Library entries retrieved by exact title:

- **“行业研究端到端工作流：从陌生赛道到 IC 立项报告（8 步 + 行业路由表）”** — adopted lifecycle-first framing, industry-specific routing, primary/secondary validation, and demand/transaction verification. The broader IC-report steps remain outside this map skill.
- **“Anthropic行业概览：快速摸清一个赛道的全景”** — adopted explicit scope/purpose/universe, market-definition discipline, value-accrual analysis, competitive dynamics, freshness dating, and realistic rather than promotional TAM treatment.
- **“3 面 10 点行研框架：直接对应 IC 立项报告章节”** — adopted macro orientation, industry lifecycle, value-chain profit allocation, bargaining power, concentration discipline, and named opportunity/risk. Company deep dives and IC chapter production remain separate workflows.

The benchmark improves research quality without changing the product: the deliverable is still a true upstream-to-downstream interactive map with node-level explainers, not a generic industry report.

## Define the boundary

Write a one-sentence product definition and an explicit included/excluded list. Resolve vendor terminology before sizing or mapping. Also record:

- the decision purpose (newcomer learning, initial screening, target-company context, or refresh);
- geography and time horizon;
- whether the universe includes public companies, private companies, or both;
- lifecycle stage and the evidence behind that judgment;
- present demand state and the narrow market definition used for any size/share claim.

Treat lifecycle, policy, market size, valuation, and investment recommendation as orientation or analytical lenses. They must never become upstream/downstream stages.

## Build the chain from transactions

For each proposed stage, ask:

1. What input is purchased or received?
2. What physical, technical, or commercial transformation occurs?
3. What product, service, or right leaves the stage?
4. Who is the immediate customer, and who ultimately pays?
5. What observable transaction proves this relationship exists?

If these questions cannot be answered, the item may be a theme, capability, risk, or investment lens rather than a chain stage.

Build the first draft from the end market backward. Starting with supply alone often produces a technology taxonomy that never proves who buys the output. The final map should include the demand trigger at the downstream end and the scarce or enabling input at the upstream end.

## Explain transmission between nodes

An edge is not merely a line. State:

- what moves across it (material, component, license, service, demand signal, or standard);
- which party sets or negotiates price;
- how lead time, capacity, yield, utilization, regulation, or demand shocks pass through;
- whether pass-through is immediate, delayed, capped by contracts, or absorbed in margin;
- the evidence source for the relationship.

If a relationship is only inferred, say so. Avoid directional arrows that are visually plausible but economically unverified.

## Separate support layers

Software, standards, certification, distribution, and infrastructure may support several stages simultaneously. Use a `support` stage and `support` edges rather than forcing the capability into one linear column.

## Classify companies

- Supplier: sells an input to the node.
- Product vendor: owns or sells the node's principal product.
- Integrator/OEM: turns components into a deployable system.
- Software/ecosystem: enables development, management, or interoperability.
- Direct customer/operator: purchases and operates the product.
- Service provider: monetizes the output downstream.

Attach evidence status. A company operating in an adjacent market is not automatically a participant. A named target customer is not a verified customer.

For each company, make the role specific enough to test: “supplier of X into Y,” “operator of Z,” or “reported PoC partner” is better than “industry leader.” Company-role evidence should resolve to a product page, filing, customer/supplier announcement, procurement record, technical document, or another source that actually supports that role.

## Route research by industry

Use the generic map structure, but change the metrics and commercialization tests:

- **Semiconductors/hardware:** process node, yield, performance per watt, design wins versus mass production, tape-out cadence, packaging, capacity and lead time.
- **Advanced manufacturing/materials:** qualification cycle, utilization, yield, throughput, scrap, capacity additions, price pass-through, customer concentration.
- **Software/AI/SaaS:** deployed workload, retention/expansion, gross margin, inference or delivery cost, switching cost, ecosystem integration, procurement owner.
- **Biotech/medical:** development stage, clinical endpoint, probability of success, regulatory path, reimbursement, manufacturing and IP/FTO.
- **Consumer/services:** channel economics, repeat purchase, store/unit economics, traffic acquisition, inventory turns, price elasticity.
- **Infrastructure/energy:** installed capacity, utilization, unit cost/LCOE, offtake or contract term, permitting, capex cycle, grid or network constraints.

When the industry does not fit a route, derive metrics from its commercialization bottleneck rather than choosing the nearest template.

## Size and concentration carefully

Market size is orientation, not a chain stage. Define the unit, geography, included revenue pool, base year, and whether the figure is shipments, bookings, installed base, or recognized revenue. When material to the task, triangulate a top-down estimate with a bottom-up driver model and explain the gap.

Use CR4/CR10 only when the numerator companies and denominator market share the same scope. Otherwise describe concentration qualitatively and flag the data gap. Do not treat thresholds such as “large market + high growth” as investment conclusions without checking access, value capture, and exit relevance.

## Map node economics

For each node, distinguish:

- **Monetization:** unit sale, subscription, license, usage, service, take rate, or contracted capacity.
- **Cost drivers:** materials, labor, compute, depreciation, yield loss, channel, customer acquisition, regulatory or qualification expense.
- **Bargaining power:** concentration, switching cost, scarcity, qualification lock-in, standards control, balance-sheet intensity, and alternative suppliers/customers.
- **Profit-pool logic:** why returns accrue here—or why competition and pass-through prevent them from doing so.

Do not infer attractive economics from technical importance alone.

## Write the investment view

Use four components:

- **Thesis:** why value may accrue here.
- **Moat:** what makes excess returns defensible.
- **Catalyst:** what could cause adoption, utilization, pricing, or market structure to change.
- **Risk:** the most plausible mechanism for thesis failure.
- **Watch:** the next observable fact that should update the view.

Avoid unsupported rankings and invented scores. Prefer decision-relevant metrics.

## Control evidence quality

Use evidence in this order when available:

1. Standards, regulators, government, filings, and audited reports.
2. Company product documentation and official announcements.
3. Credible industry research and specialist publications.
4. Interviews, databases, and news.
5. Analyst inference, explicitly labeled.

Record access dates. Refresh company, product, capacity, financing, and customer claims before delivery in fast-moving markets.

For consequential claims, seek two independent evidence paths when feasible. Examples include market size/share, production deployment, major-customer status, capacity, pricing, and regulatory approval. If triangulation is not available, preserve the claim but label the limitation instead of manufacturing certainty.
